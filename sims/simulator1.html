The Theory Behind the Oil Drop Experiment

Originally performed by Lord Rayleigh and Agnes Pockels, the Oil Drop Experiment is a brilliant, classical physics method used to determine the exact length of a single molecule using only macro-scale measurements (rulers and graduated cylinders).

When a tiny drop of oleic acid (a fatty acid found in olive oil) is placed on a basin of water dusted with lycopodium powder, it spreads rapidly outward. It continues to spread until it physically cannot spread any thinner. At this point, the patch on the water is a monolayer—a flat cylinder of oil that is exactly one molecule thick.

The Formula Used

The mathematical magic of this experiment lies in the Conservation of Volume. The volume of the initial spherical drop is completely equal to the volume of the large, flat cylindrical patch.

  • Volume of Spherical Drop (V): V = 4/3 π (d/2)3
  • Area of Flat Patch (A): A = π (D/2)2
  • Thickness of one molecule (t): Since the volume of a cylinder is V = A × t, we can rearrange this to find the thickness: t = V / A.

By dividing the small volume (in cm3) by the large area (in cm2), the result gives us the height of the molecule in centimeters, which typically equates to about 2.5 nanometers for Oleic Acid.

Real World Applications

Engineers and scientists use the properties of monolayers in several modern applications:

  • Water Conservation: In arid regions, safe chemical monolayers are spread across the surface of large reservoirs. Being one molecule thick, it uses very little material, but it creates a physical barrier that drastically reduces water evaporation.
  • Biological Cell Membranes: Every living cell is encased in a lipid bilayer (two monolayers stacked back-to-back). Understanding how molecules naturally align themselves on water helps biologists understand cellular structure.
  • Anti-Reflective Coatings: By depositing a thin film exactly one molecule thick onto glasses or camera lenses, optical engineers can cancel out specific wavelengths of light glare via destructive interference.
