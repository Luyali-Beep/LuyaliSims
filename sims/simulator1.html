How to Use This Simulator

Use the sliders to adjust either the Droplet Diameter (d) or the Patch Diameter (D). Because the molecular length of oleic acid is an unvarying physical constant, modifying one dimension computes the conjugate value instantly.

Click Execute Experiment to launch the 3D falling drop. Upon hitting the water, it spreads outwards into a thin monolayer disc. Left-click/drag on the scene to orbit, scroll to zoom in close to see the individual molecular instances, and right-drag to pan.

The Theory Behind the Oil Drop Experiment

Devised by Lord Rayleigh and refined by Agnes Pockels, this classical experiment allows one to estimate the dimension of a single molecule using basic laboratory apparatus (pipettes and shallow basins).

Oleic acid molecules possess a hydrophilic (water-attracting) carboxyl head and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) hydrocarbon tail. When placed onto water dusted with lycopodium powder, the molecules stand vertically side-by-side, forming a layer exactly one molecule thick.

The Formula Used

The calculation is rooted in the Principle of Conservation of Volume:

  • Volume of the Drop (V): V = 4/3 π (d/2)3
  • Area of the Patch (A): A = π (D/2)2
  • Thickness of one molecule (t): Modeling the monolayer as a flat cylinder of height t, V = A × t ⇒ t = V / A.

Typical experimental results yield approximately 2.5 × 10-7 cm (2.5 nm), establishing nanoscale measurements without electron microscopy.

Guided Exercises for Students

Task 1: Conservation of Volume Across Geometries

Objective: Prove that the volumetric displacement of oil remains identical regardless of geometric transformation.
Action: Set a droplet diameter of d = 0.50 mm and click "Execute Experiment". Orbit the camera to view both the reference droplet and the spread basin.
Observation & Analysis: The oil transforms from a compact 3D sphere into a broad 2D disc. Despite this radical change in shape, the total molecular volume is completely conserved: Vdrop = Vpatch = A × t

Task 2: Invariance of Single-Molecule Height (t)

Objective: Demonstrate why molecular thickness does not depend on the macroscopic volume used.
Action: Sweep the Droplet Diameter (d) slider across multiple values (e.g., 0.30 mm, 0.60 mm, and 0.90 mm). Observe the Molecule Length (t) readout in the Real-Time Analysis HUD.
Observation & Analysis: Notice that the calculated thickness remains fixed at 2.50 nm. Because molecular length is a physical constant determined by chemical bonds (the 18-carbon chain of oleic acid), adding more oil merely enlarges the surface area (A) rather than stacking molecules into thicker layers.

Task 3: Non-Linear Scaling Law (D ∝ d1.5)

Objective: Investigate the geometric relationship between the drop diameter (d) and patch diameter (D).
Action: Double the droplet diameter from 0.40 mm to 0.80 mm, and record the corresponding patch diameter (D) in both states.
Observation & Analysis: When d doubles (2×), the patch diameter does not simply double; it increases by a factor of approximately 21.5 ≈ 2.83 times! Since V ∝ d3 and A = (π/4) D2 = V / t ∝ d3 ⇒ D ∝ d3/2 (or d1.5) This non-linear scaling demonstrates how three-dimensional cubic volume expansion drives two-dimensional surface dispersion.

Task 4: Manual Unit Conversion & Thickness Calculation

Objective: Master laboratory unit conversions from millimetres and centimetres to nanometres.
Action: Set d = 0.60 mm and note the resulting patch diameter D = 23.9 cm. Calculate the thickness by hand using standard scientific notation without looking at the final metric box:
1. Find volume: V = 4/3 π (0.06 cm / 2)3 cm3
2. Find area: A = π (23.9 cm / 2)2 cm2
3. Find thickness: t = (V / A) cm, then multiply by 107 to convert into nanometres (nm).
Observation & Analysis: Compare your manual step-by-step result with the HUD readout to verify how microscopic dimensions are deduced purely from macro-scale ruler measurements.

Task 5: Evaluating Experimental Assumptions & Error Sources

Objective: Identify critical assumptions made during the physical laboratory experiment.
Action: Zoom closely into the holographic patch molecules in the 3D scene and compare the ideal simulation with a real water trough.
Observation & Analysis: The calculation assumes three major physical conditions:

  • Strict Monolayer: Assumes oil spreads until it is exactly 1 molecule thick with no overlapping bilayers or unspread lenses.
  • Geometric Packing: Assumes molecules are tightly packed, upright cylinders with zero voids or inter-molecular gaps.
  • Circular Symmetry: Assumes surface tension and lycopodium dust resistance produce a perfect circle rather than an irregular polygon.

Critical Thinking: If surface contamination on the water prevents the oil from spreading fully, would your experimental estimate for molecular thickness (t) be larger or smaller than the true value? (Answer: Larger, because a smaller measured area A in t = V / A artificially inflates the computed thickness).

Real World Applications

  • Reservoir Water Conservation: Monomolecular films spread over large open reservoirs significantly suppress evaporation losses in dry climates without cutting off aquatic aeration.
  • Biological Bilayers: Cell membranes are lipid bilayers consisting of two opposing monolayers, functioning as selective barriers in every living organism.
  • Anti-Reflective Optics: Langmuir-Blodgett monolayers applied to lenses create destructive interference for incoming glare.
