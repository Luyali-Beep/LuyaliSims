How to Operate the Simulator

This virtual laboratory provides a complete 3D interactive model of an oleic acid monolayer film on a water surface. Use the following steps to explore the experiment:

  • Droplet Diameter Slider: Adjust the diameter (d) of the initial spherical drop in millimetres. Notice how increasing the drop volume automatically recalculates the conjugate patch diameter needed to maintain a constant single-molecule height.
  • Spread Patch Diameter Slider: Alternately modify the spread monolayer diameter (D) in centimetres. The simulator will back-calculate the required spherical droplet diameter.
  • Execute Experiment Button: Triggers the dynamic delivery of the micro-droplet. Watch the 3D droplet drop onto the water, generating a surface tension shockwave, pushing aside lycopodium particles, and dispersing into a flat monomolecular disc.
  • Camera Control: Left-click and drag across the viewport to orbit the camera, scroll to zoom in close to see individual oleic acid molecular chains, and right-click drag to pan the camera target.

Scientific Theory & Working Principles

Long before the invention of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or atomic force microscopy (AFM), nineteenth-century physicists sought accessible empirical methods to determine the physical size of molecules. In 1890, Lord Rayleigh, building on observations made by German independent scientist Agnes Pockels, devised the oil-on-water monolayer technique.

The experiment exploits the amphiphilic nature of oleic acid (C17H33COOH). Each oleic acid molecule possesses two chemically distinct regions:

  • A hydrophilic carboxyl head group (-COOH) that forms strong hydrogen bonds with underlying water molecules.
  • A hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail (C17H33-) that repels polar water and extends vertically into the air.

When a diluted drop of oleic acid dissolved in a volatile solvent (such as ethanol or petroleum ether) is placed onto a surface lightly dusted with lycopodium powder, the solvent rapidly evaporates. The oleic acid molecules disperse across the surface, repelling the lycopodium powder radially outward. Because the hydrophobic tails repel water while heads cling to it, molecules cannot easily form multi-layered stacks; instead, they stand shoulder-to-shoulder, creating a film exactly one molecule thick (a monomolecular layer or monolayer).

Mathematical Foundations & Formulas

The quantitative foundation of this experiment relies directly on the Law of Conservation of Volume. The volume of the initial macroscopic sphere must equal the volume of the resulting macroscopic cylindrical disc:

Formula 1: Volume of Spherical Droplet (V)
V = (4 / 3) x π x r³ = (4 / 3) x π x (d / 2)³ = (π / 6) x d³

Formula 2: Area of Circular Monolayer Patch (A)
A = π x R² = π x (D / 2)² = (π / 4) x D²

Formula 3: Thickness of One Molecule (t)
V_drop = V_patch = A x t ==> t = V / A

Formula 4: Solution Dilution Correction (Laboratory Practice)
If an oleic acid solution is diluted in two stages by ratio 1:N each time:
Concentration (C) = (1 / N) x (1 / N) = 1 / N²
Actual Volume of Pure Oleic Acid = V_drop x C

Because the monolayer is one molecule thick, the calculated cylindrical thickness t represents the longitudinal length of an oleic acid molecule (approximately 2.5 x 10⁻⁷ cm or 2.5 nm).

Real-World Engineering & Industrial Applications

The principles underlying monomolecular spreading and self-assembly extend far beyond classical classroom demonstrations:

  • Langmuir-Blodgett Thin Films in Electronics: In modern semiconductor manufacturing, molecular monolayers are transferred onto solid substrates to construct ultrathin dielectric gates, organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), and molecular switches.
  • Evaporation Suppression in Water Reservoirs: Spreading biodegradable monomolecular films (such as cetyl alcohol) over arid-region open-air reservoirs reduces water evaporation by up to 35% without blocking dissolved oxygen transfer necessary for aquatic ecosystems.
  • Biological Membranes & Targeted Drug Delivery: Cellular life depends on lipid bilayers—two opposing monolayers functioning as selectively permeable membranes. Synthetic liposomes engineered from lipid monolayers are used to encapsulate pharmaceutical drugs, targeting specific cell receptors.
  • Lubrication and Tribology: High-performance boundary lubricants depend on single-molecule surfactant layers chemically bonded to metal gears, preventing catastrophic metal-on-metal wear during cold starts.

Practice Problems & Diagnostic Tasks

Test your quantitative comprehension using the five laboratory exercises below:

Task 1: Basic Parameter Calculation

A micro-pipette produces an oleic acid droplet with a diameter of d = 0.48 mm. When placed on water, it spreads into a circular film with diameter D = 17.5 cm. Calculate the volume of the droplet in cm³, the area of the patch in cm², and the resulting molecular thickness in nanometres (nm).

Task 2: Proportional Scaling Law

Assuming the physical length of an oleic acid molecule (t) remains constant at 2.50 nm, demonstrate algebraically how the patch diameter (D) scales with droplet diameter (d). If a student doubles the droplet diameter from 0.40 mm to 0.80 mm, by what numerical factor will the patch diameter increase?

Task 3: Dilution Series Correction

In a school lab, 1.0 cm³ of pure oleic acid is dissolved in alcohol to make 500 cm³ of solution. Then, 1.0 cm³ of this solution is diluted again to 500 cm³ with alcohol. A burette delivers 40 drops of this second mixture per cm³. One drop is dispensed onto lycopodium powder, forming a patch of diameter 14.0 cm. Calculate the estimated length of an oleic acid molecule.

Task 4: Reverse Engineering Required Volume

A researcher wishes to coat an optical wafer of diameter D = 30.0 cm with a monolayer of a specialized surfactant whose molecular length is known to be t = 1.80 nm. Assuming 100% coverage with zero voids, what volume of surfactant (in mm³) must be dispensed?

Task 5: Systematic Error Analysis

A student performs the experiment and calculates a molecular thickness of t = 5.60 nm, which is more than double the accepted literature value (~2.5 nm). Name two plausible experimental systematic errors that could cause the calculated thickness to be significantly greater than the actual value, and explain the physical mechanism for each.

Solution to Task 1:

1. Convert droplet diameter to cm: d = 0.48 mm = 0.048 cm.
Radius r = 0.048 / 2 = 0.024 cm.
Volume V = (4 / 3) x π x (0.024)³ ≈ 5.79 x 10⁻⁵ cm³ (or 0.0579 mm³).
2. Area of circular patch: Radius R = 17.5 / 2 = 8.75 cm.
Area A = π x (8.75)² ≈ 240.53 cm².
3. Thickness t = V / A = (5.79 x 10⁻⁵ cm³) / (240.53 cm²) ≈ 2.407 x 10⁻⁷ cm.
Convert to nanometres: (2.407 x 10⁻⁷ cm) x (10⁷ nm / cm) = 2.41 nm.

Solution to Task 2:

1. Droplet volume: V = (π / 6) x d³.
2. Patch area: A = (π / 4) x D².
3. Since t = V / A, we have: (π / 6) x d³ = [(π / 4) x D²] x t.
Rearranging for D:
D² = [4 x (π / 6) x d³] / (π x t) = (2 / 3t) x d³ ==> D ∝ d^(1.5) (or d^(3/2)).
4. If d is doubled (factor of 2):
New diameter D_new = D_old x 2^(1.5) = D_old x 2.828.
The patch diameter increases by a factor of 2.83 (or 2√2).

Solution to Task 3:

1. First dilution ratio = 1 / 500.
Second dilution ratio = 1 / 500.
Overall concentration C = (1 / 500) x (1 / 500) = 1 / 250,000.
2. Volume of one solution drop = 1 cm³ / 40 = 0.025 cm³.
3. Actual volume of pure oleic acid in one drop:
V_acid = 0.025 cm³ / 250,000 = 1.00 x 10⁻⁷ cm³.
4. Patch Area: Radius R = 14.0 / 2 = 7.0 cm.
A = π x (7.0)² = 49π ≈ 153.94 cm².
5. Molecular thickness t = V_acid / A = (1.00 x 10⁻⁷ cm³) / 153.94 cm² ≈ 6.50 x 10⁻¹⁰ cm.
Convert to nanometres: 6.50 x 10⁻¹⁰ cm x 10⁷ nm/cm = 0.0065 nm (or 6.5 x 10⁻³ nm).
Note: In practical laboratory settings, if the patch area is smaller than expected due to excessive lycopodium dust resistance or premature evaporation, calculated values deviate, highlighting the need for carefully calibrated dilutions.

Solution to Task 4:

1. Wafer radius R = 30.0 / 2 = 15.0 cm.
Wafer Area A = π x (15.0)² = 225π ≈ 706.86 cm².
2. Convert thickness to cm: t = 1.80 nm = 1.80 x 10⁻⁷ cm.
3. Required volume V = A x t = 706.86 cm² x (1.80 x 10⁻⁷ cm) ≈ 1.272 x 10⁻⁴ cm³.
4. Convert cm³ to mm³ (1 cm³ = 1000 mm³):
V = 1.272 x 10⁻⁴ x 1000 = 0.127 mm³.

Solution to Task 5:

Since t = V / A, any error that causes the measured area A to be smaller than the true spread, or the volume V to be overestimated, results in an inflated thickness t:
1. Incomplete Spreading (Bi-layer / Lens Formation): If the water surface is contaminated with trace oils or if excess lycopodium powder resists surface expansion, the oleic acid cannot spread completely into a single-molecule layer. Pockets remain 2 or more molecules thick, shrinking the measured area A and causing t to be calculated as significantly larger than normal.
2. Premature Solvent Evaporation in Pipette: If the volatile carrier solvent evaporates while inside the burette tip prior to dispensing, the delivered drop contains a higher concentration of pure oleic acid than calculated in V_drop x C. Using the nominal smaller concentration underestimates pure volume, leading to an erroneously high calculated value for t.
