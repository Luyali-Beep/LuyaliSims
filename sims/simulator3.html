How to Operate the Simulator

This virtual laboratory recreates the operational physics and controls of an analog/digital Cathode Ray Oscilloscope (CRO) capable of measuring both AC and DC potentials:

  • Signal Mode Selection: Choose between AC Signal (alternating voltage) and DC Voltage (steady direct potential). In AC mode, select Sinusoidal, Square, or Triangular waveforms and adjust the Peak Voltage (V_p) and Frequency (f). In DC mode, vary the DC Voltage Level (V_dc) or use the preset polarity buttons (+6.0 V, 0 V GND, -6.0 V).
  • Y-Sensitivity Calibration (Volts/Div): Controls the vertical attenuator/amplifier. Decreasing Volts/Div expands the trace vertically to resolve low voltages; increasing it compresses large voltages to fit within the graticule window.
  • Time-Base Calibration (Time/Div): Controls the horizontal sweep oscillator speed. Lower values (e.g., 0.5 ms/div) spread a single wave cycle across multiple horizontal squares, while higher values (e.g., 5.0 ms/div) display multiple cycles simultaneously. In DC mode, changing the time base does not alter the vertical deflection because DC potential remains steady across time.
  • Ground Baseline Shift (Y-Shift): Use the Y-Shift slider to relocate the 0 V reference line, modeling the physical vertical position potentiometer on a bench instrument.
  • Freeze & Fullscreen Controls: Click Freeze Trace to hold the animated phosphor sweep in place for stationary graticule counting. Use Fullscreen during lectures or laboratory displays.

Scientific Theory & Working Principles

The Cathode Ray Oscilloscope is a high-speed vector graph-plotting instrument. Unlike mechanical moving-coil meters, which suffer from mechanical inertia and low frequency limits, a CRO utilizes an electron beam with negligible mass, responding almost instantaneously to microsecond potential changes.

1. The Electron Gun Assembly

Located in the evacuated neck of the cathode ray tube (CRT), an indirect heater warms a barium/strontium oxide coated cathode, liberating electrons via thermionic emission. A negatively biased control grid surrounds the cathode to modulate electron flow, controlling trace intensity. Successive cylindrical anodes maintained at positive potentials of 1 kV to 20 kV accelerate and collimate the beam into an axial ray.

2. Electrostatic Deflection Plates

The electron ray passes between two perpendicular pairs of parallel conducting plates:

  • Vertical Deflection (Y-Plates): Connected to the signal under test through a calibrated attenuator. A potential difference creates a uniform electric field E = V_y / d, exerting a transverse electrostatic force F = e x E. This deflects the beam vertically by a distance directly proportional to the applied test voltage.
  • Horizontal Deflection (X-Plates): Fed by an internal relaxation oscillator generating a linear sawtooth voltage ramp. The beam is drawn smoothly across the screen from left to right at constant velocity, then quickly returns (flyback) during a blanked interval.

3. Phosphor Screen Luminescence

The front face of the tube is coated internally with zinc sulfide or silicate phosphors. When high-velocity electrons strike the phosphor layer, their kinetic energy excites orbital electrons into higher states. Upon radiative de-excitation, visible light is emitted via fluorescence (immediate emission) and phosphorescence (persistence), producing a bright glowing trace.

4. Direct Current vs. Alternating Current Coupling

When measuring DC potentials, the electron beam experiences a constant upward or downward force. With the time base active, this draws a straight horizontal line offset from the 0 V center axis by an amount d = V_dc / (Volts/Div). When measuring AC potentials, the alternating voltage swings the beam cyclically above and below the baseline, plotting the signal's full wave shape against time.

Mathematical Foundations & Formulas

Quantitative analysis on an oscilloscope requires converting visual division counts into dimensional physical units (Volts and Seconds) using calibrated dial ratios:

Direct Current Voltage: V_dc = (Vertical Deflection from 0V Baseline in Divisions) x (Volts / Div)
Peak-to-Peak AC Voltage: V_pp = (Vertical Divisions Peak-to-Trough) x (Volts / Div)
Peak AC Voltage (Amplitude): V_p = V_pp / 2
Root-Mean-Square Voltage: V_rms = V_p / √2 = V_p x 0.7071 (for sinusoidal signals)
Time Period for One Cycle: T = (Horizontal Divisions for 1 Full Cycle) x (Time / Div)
Signal Frequency: f = 1 / T (Hertz, where T is expressed in seconds)
Angular Frequency: ω = 2 x π x f (radians per second)

Real-World Industrial & Engineering Applications

The CRO and its modern digital storage equivalent (DSO) serve as foundational instruments across science and engineering:

  • Medical Biomedical Instrumentation (ECG & EEG): Electrocardiographs use differential oscilloscope amplifiers to plot millivolt-level cardiac action potentials over time, enabling diagnosis of arrhythmias and myocardial infarctions.
  • Automotive Electronic Control Unit (ECU) Diagnostics: Technicians inspect inductive crankshaft sensor pulses, fuel injector pulse-width modulation (PWM), and CAN bus line voltages to detect timing issues and electrical noise.
  • Telecommunications & Digital Bus Testing: RF engineers use eye diagrams on high-bandwidth oscilloscopes to verify signal integrity, measuring rise times, jitter, and propagation delays on multi-gigahertz serial buses.
  • Acoustic Analysis & Power Electronics: Audio engineers observe clipping and harmonic distortion in power amplifiers, while electrical power engineers inspect mains harmonics, power factors, and inverter switching transients.

Practice Problems & Diagnostic Tasks

Test your quantitative comprehension using the five laboratory exercises below:

Task 1: Direct Current (DC) Battery Cell Measurement

A battery cell of unknown electromotive force (EMF) is connected to a CRO with the time base running. Before connection, the zero-volt ground line is centered on the middle graticule axis. Upon connection, the horizontal trace shifts steadily upwards by 3.2 divisions. The Y-sensitivity control is calibrated to 0.5 V/div.

(a) Calculate the measured DC voltage (V_dc) of the cell.

(b) State the polarity of the terminal connected to the active input.

(c) If the Y-sensitivity dial is changed to 2.0 V/div, what will be the new vertical deflection in divisions?

Task 2: AC Sinusoidal Voltage & RMS Calculation

An AC step-down transformer secondary is connected to a CRO. The sinusoidal waveform spans 5.6 vertical divisions from its lowest trough to its highest crest. The Y-sensitivity knob is set to 5.0 V/div.

(a) Calculate the peak-to-peak voltage (V_pp).

(b) Determine the peak amplitude (V_p).

(c) Calculate the root-mean-square voltage (V_rms) that a standard digital AC voltmeter would measure across this secondary winding.

Task 3: Periodic Time & Frequency Determination

A function generator produces a square-wave clock signal. On the CRO display, exactly 2 complete cycles occupy a horizontal width of 8.0 divisions when the Time-Base is set to 0.5 ms/div.

(a) Determine the horizontal distance in divisions occupied by one single cycle.

(b) Calculate the periodic time (T) of the clock signal in seconds.

(c) Determine the fundamental operating frequency (f) of the circuit in Hertz.

Task 4: Graticule Boundary & Voltage Clipping Prevention

A signal generator outputs a symmetrical sinusoidal voltage with an amplitude V_p = 16.0 V. The CRO screen has 8 total vertical divisions (4 above the center axis, 4 below).

(a) If an operator selects a Y-sensitivity of 2.0 V/div, calculate the total peak-to-peak deflection in divisions. Will the waveform fit on the screen?

(b) Which standard Y-sensitivity setting (chosen from: 1.0 V/div, 2.0 V/div, 5.0 V/div, or 10.0 V/div) should the operator choose to maximize vertical trace height without clipping off-screen?

Task 5: Multi-Step Audio Tone Diagnostic

An acoustic transducer picks up a musical concert pitch tone. On the oscilloscope, 4 complete cycles span 9.1 divisions horizontally with the Time-Base set to 1.0 ms/div. Vertically, the wave occupies 4.4 divisions with a Y-sensitivity of 2.0 V/div.

(a) Calculate the period (T) of one sound wave cycle.

(b) Calculate the frequency of the sound tone and identify if it is near the standard musical tuning note Concert A (440 Hz).

(c) Calculate the peak-to-peak voltage (V_pp) and the RMS voltage (V_rms) of the microphone output.

Solution to Task 1:

(a) V_dc = (Vertical Divisions) x (Volts / Div) = 3.2 div x 0.5 V/div = +1.60 V.

(b) Because the beam is deflected upward toward the positive upper Y-deflection plate, the active input is connected to the Positive (+) terminal.

(c) At 2.0 V/div: Deflection = V_dc / (Volts / Div) = 1.60 V / (2.0 V/div) = 0.8 divisions upward.

Solution to Task 2:

(a) V_pp = (Vertical Divisions) x (Volts / Div) = 5.6 div x 5.0 V/div = 28.0 V.

(b) V_p = V_pp / 2 = 28.0 V / 2 = 14.0 V.

(c) V_rms = V_p / √2 = 14.0 V / 1.4142 = 9.90 V (approximately 10 V RMS).

Solution to Task 3:

(a) 2 cycles occupy 8.0 divisions, so 1 cycle occupies: 8.0 div / 2 = 4.0 divisions.

(b) Periodic time T = 4.0 div x 0.5 ms/div = 2.0 ms = 0.002 s (or 2.0 x 10^-3 s).

(c) Frequency f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.002 s = 500 Hz.

Solution to Task 4:

(a) Peak-to-peak voltage V_pp = 2 x V_p = 2 x 16.0 V = 32.0 V. At 2.0 V/div, required divisions = 32.0 V / (2.0 V/div) = 16.0 divisions. Because the screen only has 8 total divisions, the trace clips off-screen by 8 divisions (4 above and 4 below).

(b) At 5.0 V/div: 32.0 V / (5.0 V/div) = 6.4 divisions. Since 6.4 divisions is less than the 8.0 available divisions and leaves 0.8 divisions of headroom above and below, 5.0 V/div is the optimal calibration to maximize measurement resolution without clipping.

Solution to Task 5:

(a) Horizontal span per cycle = 9.1 div / 4 = 2.275 divisions. Period T = 2.275 div x 1.0 ms/div = 2.275 ms = 0.002275 s.

(b) Frequency f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.002275 s = 439.56 Hz (approximately 440 Hz). Yes, this represents the standard Concert A (440 Hz) pitch.

(c) V_pp = 4.4 div x 2.0 V/div = 8.80 V. Peak V_p = 8.80 / 2 = 4.40 V. V_rms = 4.40 / √2 = 3.11 V.
