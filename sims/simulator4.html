How to Use This Simulator

This interactive laboratory allows you to safely experiment with the internal physics of an evacuated X-ray tube:

  • HOLD TO EXPOSE (Top Button): Hold this button to apply the high-voltage potential. Electrons immediately accelerate across the high vacuum and slam into the angled tungsten target.
  • Tube Current (mA): Drag the slider to heat the cathode. Notice the filament coil glowing hotter and the localized blue space-charge electron cloud growing denser inside the focusing cup.
  • Accelerating Potential (kVp): Controls pulling force. Higher kVp accelerates electrons to immense velocities, projecting higher frequency, deeply penetrating X-ray photons downwards through the port.
  • Rotating Anode: Disabling the motor halts target rotation. Watch the temperature bar surge! Without rotation spreading the 99% thermal yield along the circular track, the spot melts and destroys the tube.
  • Hover / Touch Inspection: Hover over any part (coils, target face, rotor, bottom port) to reveal its composition and engineering function.

The Theory Behind X-Ray Production

X-rays are produced when fast-moving electrons collide with high-density matter inside an evacuated glass envelope.

1. Thermionic Emission & Space Charge

At the cathode, a step-down circuit heats a coiled Tungsten filament wire beyond 2,200°C. Outer-shell electrons boil off into the vacuum, forming an electrostatic space charge held in a concentrated bundle by the negatively charged nickel focusing cup.

2. Vacuum Acceleration & Anode Impact

Because the tube is deeply evacuated (free of air molecules that would cause collisions and electrical arcing), the high-voltage potential difference propels electrons cleanly toward the positive anode at roughly half the speed of light.

  • Bremsstrahlung (Braking): High-speed electrons decelerate around heavy tungsten nuclei (Z=74), emitting lost kinetic energy as continuous-spectrum X-ray photons.
  • Characteristic Radiation: Incoming electrons eject inner-shell tungsten electrons; outer electrons cascade downward, releasing discrete characteristic photon spikes.

3. The Line-Focus Principle

By angling the target face (typically 7° to 20°), the actual focal spot struck by electrons remains large for maximum heat dissipation, while the projected effective focal spot directed downwards through the window port is small, delivering maximum radiographic sharpness.

What catastrophic failure occurs if the anode rotor is turned off during an extended high-current exposure?

Answer Key

  1. A vacuum ensures electrons travel unobstructed from cathode to anode without colliding with gas atoms, which would slow them down, destroy beam focus, and ignite destructive electrical arcs.
  2. Electrons achieve significantly higher kinetic speed. Upon impact, they produce higher-frequency, shorter-wavelength (harder) X-rays with substantially greater penetrating power through dense materials.
  3. The target is angled according to the Line-Focus Principle so that a broad, heat-resistant electron impact track translates into a narrow, high-resolution effective focal spot projected straight downwards through the Beryllium window.
  4. Power = Voltage × Current = 100,000 V × 0.3 A = 30,000 Watts (30 kW) of instant electrical energy.
  5. Without rotation, the 99% thermal heat cannot be distributed along a circular track. Heat concentrates on a single point, exceeding Tungsten's melting point of 3,422°C, causing focal track pitting, cracking, and vacuum envelope destruction.
