Scientific Theory & Functions of Components

The interactive simulator models macroscopic electromagnetic principles based on Kirchhoff's Laws and Ohm's Law. Below are the functional definitions of the components available in the palette:

  • Power Sources (DC/AC): Provide the necessary electromotive force (voltage) to push electrons continuously through the circuit.
  • Resistors: Introduce controlled resistance to limit current flow, forming the basis of voltage dividers and current limiters.
  • Potentiometers / Rheostats: Variable resistors equipped with an adjustable wiper, permitting manual control of circuit resistance, useful for tuning and calibration.
  • Semiconductor Diodes: Act as electrical one-way valves. They permit current flow in a forward direction (after overcoming a ~0.6V threshold) while blocking reverse flow.
  • Transformers: Utilize electromagnetic induction across coupled coils to step-up or step-down alternating current (AC) voltages safely.
  • Capacitors: Store energy dynamically within an electrostatic field. They effectively block DC currents while permitting alternating AC signals to pass.
  • Measuring Instruments:
    • Voltmeter: Measures potential difference. It features exceptionally high internal resistance and is connected in parallel to avoid altering circuit flow.
    • Ammeter: Measures current throughput. It features near-zero internal resistance and is connected directly in series.
    • CRO (Oscilloscope): Visualizes voltage variations over time as continuous waveforms, acting as an essential diagnostic tool for AC frequency analysis.

Mathematical Foundations & Formulas

The simulator's node-analysis engine dynamically resolves circuit matrices using these fundamental physical equations:

Ohm's Law: Voltage (V) = Current (I) x Resistance (R)
Electrical Power: Power (P) = Voltage (V) x Current (I)
Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL): Sum of Currents Entering a Node = Sum of Currents Leaving
Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL): Sum of Voltage Drops in a Closed Loop = Sum of Voltage Sources
Transformer Ratio: Primary Voltage / Secondary Voltage = Primary Turns / Secondary Turns

Practice Problems & Diagnostic Tasks

Build configurations in the simulator and verify your quantitative comprehension using these standard laboratory exercises:

Task 1: Basic Ohm's Law Calculation

A 12V DC power source is connected in series with a 24-ohm fixed resistor. Calculate the theoretical current flowing through the circuit loop.

Task 2: Transformer Voltage Scaling

A Step-Up Transformer with a turns ratio of 1:10 (Primary:Secondary) is supplied with an AC peak voltage of 5V. What is the peak voltage generated at the secondary coil?

Task 3: Voltage Divider Operation

A 100-ohm potentiometer is connected strictly across a 10V DC source. If the center wiper is adjusted precisely to the 40% mark, what is the voltage measured across the lower resistive portion?

Task 4: Diode Current Control

A silicon diode (which exhibits a standard forward voltage drop of 0.6V) is positioned in series with a 10-ohm resistor and a 5.6V DC battery. Calculate the resulting current in the circuit.

Task 5: Component Power Dissipation

A specific electronic component drawing 0.02 Amperes of current registers a voltage drop of 2.0V across its terminals. Calculate the power dissipated by the component in milliwatts.

Click to Reveal Solutions and Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs

Solution to Task 1: Apply Ohm's Law formula (Current = Voltage / Resistance). Therefore, 12 / 24 results in 0.5 Amperes.

Solution to Task 2: Use the standard transformer ratio (Secondary Voltage = Primary Voltage x (Secondary Turns / Primary Turns)). Therefore, 5 x 10 equals 50 Volts.

Solution to Task 3: The physical resistance of the lower portion equates to 40% of 100 ohms, which is 40 ohms. The voltage drop scales proportionally (Voltage = Total Voltage x (Target Resistance / Total Resistance)). Therefore, 10 x 0.4 equals 4.0 Volts.

Solution to Task 4: The effective potential driving the current is the battery voltage minus the intrinsic diode drop (5.6 - 0.6 = 5.0 Volts). Using Ohm's Law (Current = Effective Voltage / Resistance), 5.0 / 10 equals 0.5 Amperes.

Solution to Task 5: Electrical Power equates to Voltage x Current. Therefore, 2.0 x 0.02 equals 0.04 Watts. To appropriately convert to milliwatts, multiply the result by 1000, yielding 40 milliwatts.
